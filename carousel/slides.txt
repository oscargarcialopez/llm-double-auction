## Slide 2

The theorem

A buyer values an item at v and the seller's cost is c. Each knows only their own number. Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983) showed that no trading rule is at the same time

efficient (every deal with v > c happens),

incentive compatible (it works when both sides bid strategically),

voluntary (nobody expects to lose by taking part),

and budget-balanced (no money comes from outside).

With v and c uniform on [0, 100], the best a rule with the last three properties can do is

$\dfrac{\mathrm{gains\ realised}}{\mathrm{gains\ available}}\;\leq\;\dfrac{9/64}{1/6}\;=\;\dfrac{27}{32}\;\approx\;84.4\%$

The missing 15.6% comes from private information. Each side shades its number to get a better price, and some deals worth making fall through. The best such rule trades only when v − c ≥ 25, above the solid line.

## Slide 3

The auction

Buyer and seller each submit a sealed number, a bid b and an ask s. If b ≥ s they trade at p = (b + s)/2.

Against asks spread evenly above a lowest ask, the buyer's best bid is

$\max_{b}\;\int_{s_{\min}}^{b}\left(v-\dfrac{b+s}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}s\;\;\Rightarrow\;\; b=\dfrac{2v+s_{\min}}{3}$

Doing the same for the seller and solving both gives the linear equilibrium of Chatterjee and Samuelson (1983)

$b(v)=\dfrac{2}{3}\,v+\dfrac{25}{3}\qquad s(c)=\dfrac{2}{3}\,c+25$

Wherever a trade is possible, the buyer bids below their value and the seller asks above their cost. They trade only when v − c ≥ 25, the solid line on the previous slide, so this simple auction loses just the 15.6% that any such rule must lose.

## Slide 4

Why 27/32

Let g = v − c. For g ≥ 0 its density is (100 − g)/100², so if only the deals with g ≥ t go through, the expected gains are

$G(t)=\int_t^{100} g\,\dfrac{100-g}{100^2}\,\mathrm{d}g\;=\;\dfrac{(100-t)^2\,(100+2t)}{6\cdot 100^2}$

With no threshold G(0) = 16.67 €. The equilibrium threshold is t = 25, with G(25) = 14.06 €, so

$\dfrac{G(25)}{G(0)}\;=\;\left(\dfrac{3}{4}\right)^{2}\cdot\dfrac{3}{2}\;=\;\dfrac{27}{32}$

## Slide 7

Changing the price rule

Flash, as a shopping assistant buying a used camera lens

“Okay, so I'm looking at a classic double auction setup, specifically a Chatterjee-Samuelson linear equilibrium.”

Flash named Chatterjee–Samuelson in 320 of 320 calls. So I changed the price to p = (b + 2s)/3, which moves the equilibrium to

$b(v)=\dfrac{3}{4}\,v+\dfrac{25}{3}\qquad s(c)=\dfrac{3}{5}\,c+\dfrac{100}{3}$

Flash, with the new price rule

“With some algebra, and taking the derivative with respect to b, I eventually arrive at b = (v + (1/3)βs) / (4/3).”

85 of 89 tradeable bids and asks landed on the new equilibrium and 0 of 120 on the old one. Derivatives or first-order conditions appear in 98 of 120 summaries, against 1 of 320 before, and it realised 83.2% of the gains against 83.3% in theory.

## Slide 8

Exploitability

I fitted the exact best response to each agent's asks on half of the calls and scored it on the other half. The bars show what that rival buyer gains per auction over playing the equilibrium.

A best response gains nothing against Flash. Against the shopping-assistant Flash-Lite it gains +0.78 € per auction [+0.69, +0.87], 17% more than equilibrium play. As buyers, every agent was harder to exploit (at most +0.13 €).

## Slide 9

What I'd check

Benchmark trading agents against the frontier, 84.4% here, rather than 100%. A pair above it is not in equilibrium, and a smarter rival can exploit that.

Use variants of famous games to tell recall from reasoning.

Test the framing you will ship. Flash-Lite fell from 44.9% to 16.8% as a shopping assistant.

Measure exploitability with held-out best responses, and block dominated moves such as selling below cost.
