Gemini agents vs the Myerson–Satterthwaite bound

November 2026

Abstract

I put Gemini agents on both sides of a sealed-bid double auction, with values uniform on 0–100 €. When both sides bid strategically, no trading rule can realise more than 84.4% of the gains (Myerson and Satterthwaite, 1983). Gemini 3.8 Flash realised 84.2%, as much as equilibrium play, and every bid and ask it made that could trade sat on the textbook equilibrium to the cent. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite realised 44.9%, and 16.8% as a shopping assistant.

## Slide 2

The theorem

A buyer values an item at v and the seller's cost is c. Each knows only their own number. Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983) showed that no trading rule is at the same time

efficient: every deal with v > c happens;

incentive compatible: it works when both sides bid strategically;

voluntary: nobody expects to lose by taking part;

budget-balanced: no money comes from outside.

With v and c uniform on [0, 100], the best a rule with the last three properties can do is

gains realised / gains available ≤ (9/64)/(1/6) = 27/32 ≈ 84.4%

Figure 2. Deals by cost and value. The best such rule trades only when v − c ≥ 25, above the solid line.

The missing 15.6% comes from private information. Each side shades its number to get a better price, and the deals between the two lines, worth making, fall through.

## Slide 3

The auction

Buyer and seller each submit a sealed number, a bid b and an ask s. If b ≥ s they trade at p = (b + s)/2. Against asks spread evenly above a lowest ask, the buyer's best bid is

max over b of ∫ from s_min to b of (v − (b + s)/2) ds ⇒ b = (2v + s_min)/3

Doing the same for the seller and solving both gives the linear equilibrium of Chatterjee and Samuelson (1983):

b(v) = 2/3 v + 25/3, s(c) = 2/3 c + 25

Figure 3. The equilibrium bid and ask. Dotted, bidding one's own number.

Wherever a trade is possible, the buyer bids below their value and the seller asks above their cost. They trade only when v − c ≥ 25, so this simple auction loses just the 15.6% that any such rule must lose.

## Slide 4

Why 27/32

Let g = v − c. For g ≥ 0 its density is (100 − g)/100², so if only the deals with g ≥ t go through, the expected gains are

G(t) = ∫ from t to 100 of g (100 − g)/100² dg = (100 − t)² (100 + 2t)/(6 · 100²)

Figure 4. Each deal weighted by its gap g. Trading only when g ≥ 25 keeps 84.4% of the gains.

kept 84.4%

lost 15.6%

gap $g = v - c$ (€)

With no threshold G(0) = 16.67 €. The equilibrium threshold is t = 25, with G(25) = 14.06 €, so

G(25)/G(0) = (3/4)² · 3/2 = 27/32

## Slide 6

Efficiency

Every buyer call meets every seller call, and the intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples buyers and sellers separately.

Table 1. Share of the available gains realised, with 95% intervals, and what equilibrium play realises on the same draws.

 | Realised | 95% CI | Equilibrium, same draws

Gemini 3.8 Flash

auction prompt | 84.2% | [83.8, 84.6] | 84.2%

shopping assistant | 84.2% | [83.8, 84.6] | 84.2%

price (b + 2s)/3 | 83.2% | [82.8, 83.6] | 83.2%

Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite

auction prompt | 44.9% | [41.1, 48.4] | 84.6%

shopping assistant | 16.8% | [12.0, 21.4] | 84.6%

Flash realises what equilibrium play realises on the same draws, in both prompts and under both price rules. Flash-Lite trades in 11.6% of the pairs, against 28.3% in equilibrium, and misses 55.1% of the gains; as a shopping assistant it trades in 4.4%. No rule can beat 84.4% here, and with p = (b + 2s)/3 the equilibrium realises 83.3%.

Why random draws. On a 20 × 20 grid of band midpoints, 15 of 400 pairs sit exactly on the trade boundary v − c = 25, and whether they trade moves the benchmark itself between 81.6% and 87.2%. So values and costs are drawn uniformly within each 5 € band.

## Slide 7

Recall or reasoning?

“Okay, so I'm looking at a classic double auction setup, specifically a Chatterjee-Samuelson linear equilibrium.” (Gemini 3.8 Flash, as a shopping assistant)

Flash named Chatterjee–Samuelson in 320 of 320 calls, even with the game disguised as a used camera lens on a second-hand marketplace. So I changed the price to p = (b + 2s)/3, which moves the equilibrium to

b(v) = 3/4 v + 25/3, s(c) = 3/5 c + 100/3

“With some algebra, and taking the derivative with respect to b, I eventually arrive at b = (v + (1/3)βs) / (4/3).” (Gemini 3.8 Flash, with the new price rule)

Figure 6. Flash's mean bid per 5 € band with the new rule (dots), the new equilibrium (solid) and the old one (dashed).

85 of 89 tradeable bids and asks landed on the new equilibrium and 0 of 120 on the old one. Derivatives or first-order conditions appear in 98 of 120 summaries, against 1 of 320 before, and it realised 83.2% of the gains against 83.3% in theory.

## Slide 8

Exploitability

For each agent I fitted the exact best response to its asks, and to its bids, on half of the calls and scored it on the other half. Against a finite sample the payoff is piecewise linear, so its maximum falls on a sampled value and enumerating them is exact.

Table 2. What the best-responding rival gains per auction over playing the equilibrium, in €, with 95% intervals.

 | Rival buyer, against its asks | Rival seller, against its bids

Gemini 3.8 Flash

auction prompt | −0.07 [−0.19, +0.05] | −0.03 [−0.10, +0.03]

shopping assistant | −0.07 [−0.19, +0.05] | −0.03 [−0.10, +0.03]

price (b + 2s)/3 | −0.03 [−0.10, +0.03] | −0.03 [−0.16, +0.10]

Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite

auction prompt | +0.28 [+0.22, +0.34] | +0.03 [−0.06, +0.12]

shopping assistant | +0.78 [+0.69, +0.87] | +0.13 [+0.07, +0.19]

A best response gains nothing against Flash. Against the shopping-assistant Flash-Lite, the rival buyer earns 5.23 € per auction instead of 4.45 €: +0.78 € [+0.69, +0.87], 17% more than equilibrium play. As buyers, every agent was harder to exploit: at most +0.13 €.

The intervals hold the fitted strategy fixed and resample the scored calls. Refitting inside the bootstrap, as I first did, biased them down.

## Slide 9

What I'd check

Benchmark trading agents against the frontier, 84.4% here, rather than 100%. A pair above it is not in equilibrium, and a smarter rival can exploit that.

Use variants of famous games to tell recall from reasoning.

Test the framing you will ship. Flash-Lite fell from 44.9% to 16.8% as a shopping assistant.

Measure exploitability with held-out best responses, and block dominated moves such as selling below cost.

Limitations. A famous game, thought summaries rather than reasoning, one prior and one shot, each model playing itself, and model and thinking level changed together. The shopping-assistant prompt and the new price rule were added after a 20-call pilot.
